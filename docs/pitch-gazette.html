Pitch Edition: how to explain, demo and defend our prototype
Vol. 1 · No. 1Friday, 30 October 2026Prototype: github.com/Aman06-10/webvision-
Filing an RTI shouldn't cost people their money, their time, or their courage.
Hamne RTI file karne ka poora safar dubara socha: apni bhasha mein bolo, sahi office tak pahuncho, paisa kabhi gum na ho, aur ruk jaaye to appeal tak saath chalo.

The Right to Information Act gives every Indian citizen the power to ask the government for records. The law is strong. The experience of using it is not. On the central portal, a request for a state office is returned and the fee is not refunded. The text box accepts only English letters and fourteen symbols, so a villager cannot write in Hindi, let alone Tamil. If money is cut and no registration number comes, the portal tells you to wait up to two days and not try again. The first-appeal button may refuse to open, and the reasons sit inside a long FAQ.

After that comes the bigger wall. Reports on the Information Commissions say the Central Information Commission returned 14,603 cases without orders in a single year, many for format defects, and that about 3.85 lakh appeals and complaints were pending across 28 commissions in mid-2026. People who are rejected on a technicality often do not come back.

Our project is a redesign concept, not the official portal. It shows that most of these failures can be prevented by design: a voice-first drafting flow, a pre-payment office check, a payment that cannot be lost or doubled, and tools that explain the appeal process in plain words.

The pitch in one line

"We help any Indian file an RTI in their own language, to the right office, with a payment that never loses their money, and then help them appeal when it stalls."

Who is it for, and why

1. The citizen, especially rural and non-English users, who loses fee or hope to avoidable mistakes.
2. Civil society and journalists, who file in volume and need appeals that are not returned on format.
3. The government itself: fewer returned requests, fewer helpline emails, fewer lost appeals, more trust in a flagship transparency law.

Your 30-second pitch

"Every Indian has a right to ask the government for information. But the request box takes only English letters, the portal sends state requests back with no refund, and when a payment stalls it tells you to wait two days and not retry. We rebuilt the journey: speak in your own language, we check the office before you pay, a slow bank can never cost you twice, and if the request stalls we guide you through the appeal in the Commission's own format. It's a working prototype, built on the portal's own published rules."

How to say it

"This is not a guess. We read the portal's own guidelines and FAQ, and the Central Information Commission's second-appeal guidelines, and we measured the gaps against them."
"Last year the CIC returned 14,603 cases without orders. Our appeal tools are built on the Commission's own checklist, so a citizen finds the defect before filing."

Do not say

"The portal is broken." We read it; we did not test it end to end.
"Mobile is bad / CAPTCHA fails." We did not test either.
"Our checker will stop appeals being returned." That is our hypothesis, not a result.
"All 22 languages work." Only Hindi and English are verified.

The pain, in four steps of a citizen's journey

Before filing. Which office? Which portal? How do I word it? Questions that ask "why" or for an opinion are often refused, because the Act covers information in records. A person who does not read English has no way in at all.

Paying. Seven ways to fail, a registration number that may take two days, and a warning not to retry. A worried user retries anyway, and that is how fees get doubled.

Waiting. The 30-day clock is a black box. Status needs a registration number, an email, a CAPTCHA and an OTP that can arrive late.

Appealing. A refusal cites a section the user cannot read. The appeal window is easy to miss. The Commission's format is strict, and the biggest document of the whole process is the one most likely to be sent back.

The design idea in one paragraph

Put rules first and AI second. Dates, character limits, payment states and the CIC checklist are plain, testable rules, so they are predictable and cheap. AI is used where language is hard: speech, translation, understanding a request, answering questions. Every AI call has a fallback, so the demo never dead-ends. And when the AI is unsure, the app says so and lets the user choose.

The one-liner for "why this matters"

A transparency law only works if ordinary people can use it. When the front door asks for English, a card with online use switched on, and a perfect appeal format, the people it was written for are the ones left outside. This prototype shows the door can be widened without changing the law.

Three engineering choices to be proud of

1. Fallback everywhere. No AI key, a timeout or a rate limit never ends the flow. The office finder drops back to keyword rules; translation reports "unavailable" and the browser mic takes over.
2. We tested the AI and reported what failed. Several listed models were retired or not deployed for the account; Riva Translate gave Hungarian for English to Hindi until we found the exact prompt format. We kept what worked and flagged the rest.
3. Grounded, not guessed. Help answers only from our knowledge base, with sources; the appeal tools follow the CIC's own checklist.

Honest limits to state yourself

NVIDIA's hosted models are a trial service; reports say about 40 requests a minute, and NVIDIA says limits vary. We throttle at 35 per minute per service and show counts at /usage. Production would need a paid or self-hosted deployment.
Mic and speech depend on the internet.
SQLite and the mock gateway are for the demo only.
Email-verified access to "My requests" is not built yet.

Anything else: have a 3-minute screen recording ready and keep talking over it.

Judges will ask: honest answers

Yeh sawaal zaroor aayenge. Jawab chhote aur sach rakho; "humein nahi pata" bolna bhi theek hai agar saath mein bata do ki kaise pata karoge.
Is this the official portal? Does it file real RTIs?
No. It is a redesign concept. Nothing is filed and payments are simulated.
Why not just fix the existing portal?
That is the goal: these are suggestions the owners could adopt. We built a working prototype so the argument is concrete, not a slide.
How accurate are voice and translation?
Hindi and English are verified end to end, and translation between them is reliable. Other languages are beta: we found Tamil, Telugu, Marathi and Gujarati mistranslated, so the app warns and asks the user to review.
Why not Bhashini, the government's own language service?
We looked at it; its docs say it is free for non-production use. We have not tested it, because that needs an account and credentials, and we do not claim what we have not tested. The design lets a dedicated language service be plugged in; that is the route to more than two languages.
How good is the office finder?
On 26 test queries we tuned against it got 26 right; on a separate set of 18 written afterwards it got 17. The sets are small, and the 22 offices are demo data. It can answer "unknown" and shows alternatives.
Is the appeal tool legal advice?
No. It follows the Commission's published guidelines and the Act. The Commission may still return an appeal, and a late appeal can still be admitted for good reason; we do not judge that.
Does it prevent returned appeals?
Unknown. Returns are documented; our checker targets the documented causes. A pilot would measure it. We call it a hypothesis.
What about privacy? RTI users face real danger.
We store only an email, the request text and payment events, and we warn users to remove ID numbers. A real deployment needs email-verified access (not built yet), encryption, retention limits and DPDP compliance. We would design it with RTI safety groups.
Can it scale?
Not as built: SQLite, a mock gateway and trial-tier AI limits (reportedly about 40 requests a minute). The architecture separates API, rules and AI, so production moves to Postgres, a job queue and paid or self-hosted models.
Is the Help assistant reliable?
It answers only from a small knowledge base and cites sources; off-topic questions are refused. The knowledge base paraphrases the Act and portal text and still needs checking against the Act.
What did you build versus reuse?
Built: the flow, payment state machine, rules engines, appeal tools, UI and tests. Reused: NVIDIA's hosted models. Read and cited: the portal and CIC pages.
What is the DPDP Act's effect on RTI?
Section 44(3) rewrites the personal-information exemption and is contested in the Supreme Court; sources disagree on timing. We do not take a side; our reply explainer notes that clause (j) is contested.
Who would use and pay for this?
It is a public good, not a product. Natural pilots: RTI help organisations, journalists, and assisted-filing points such as common service centres. The government benefit is fewer returned requests and helpline emails.
What is next?
Email-verified access, the real office directory, more languages through a dedicated service, real notifications, an assisted mode for rural users, and a pilot to measure returned appeals.

Roadmap you can promise

Next: email-verified access; real office directory; real reminders by email, SMS and WhatsApp.
Then: more languages through a dedicated language service; assisted filing for rural users; a pilot with an RTI help group to measure returned appeals.
Long term: offer the rules (appeal checklist, character rules, wrong-office warning) as an open library so the official portals can adopt them.

Cheat sheet: facts, words and checklist

What is verified, and what is not

Verified in testing: Hindi speech to text and text to speech (round trip); Hindi and English translation; help answers grounded in sources, off-topic refused; office finder on two query sets; appeal dates at every boundary (unit tests); second-appeal checklist; payment edge cases (47 tests pass); full browser flow in English and Hindi, dark and light; contrast of all text.
Not verified or not built: real payments or filing; languages beyond Hindi and English; email or SMS reminders; email-verified access; the real office list; screen-reader testing; real-world effect on returned appeals; mobile layout of the appeal tools in Hindi.

Words you may need

RTI: Right to Information Act, 2005.
CPIO / PIO: the officer who must reply, within 30 days.
Nodal Officer: receives online requests on the portal and forwards them to the CPIO.
First appeal (FAA): free; to a senior officer in the same office; within 30 days of the reply.
Second appeal (CIC/SIC): to the Information Commission; after the first is decided or 45 days pass; within 90 days.
Section 8(1)(j): personal-information exemption, the most cited.
Section 6(3): wrong office, so the request is transferred.
BPL: below poverty line, no fee with a certificate.
Reconciliation: matching bank records to payments, which is why a number can take days.
Idempotency key: a unique tag so the same payment request can never charge twice.
Speech to text / text to speech: turning voice into words and words into voice.
